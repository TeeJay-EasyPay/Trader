Airlines trade on a distinct set of drivers that a generic equity framework
tends to underweight, because the business is unusually exposed to a few
large, largely uncontrollable input costs and to demand that is highly
cyclical and seasonal.

**Fuel costs.** Jet fuel is typically one of the largest single operating
costs for an airline, and it is a commodity input the airline does not
control. A crude oil price spike compresses margins directly and quickly,
often faster than an airline can pass the cost through via fares (fare
increases lag cost increases because of competitive and demand
constraints). Fuel-hedging programs vary significantly by airline — some
hedge a meaningful fraction of forward fuel needs, some hedge minimally —
so the same oil move can affect two airlines very differently depending on
their hedge book, which is itself real, checkable evidence, not something to
assume is uniform across the sector.

**Load factor.** The percentage of available seats actually sold on a given
flight is one of the most direct read-throughs to profitability, because an
airline's costs are largely fixed per flight regardless of how full it is —
revenue above the load factor that covers fixed costs is disproportionately
high-margin. A small change in load factor can therefore produce a
disproportionately large change in profitability; airlines and analysts
watch load-factor trends closely as a leading indicator, often ahead of
full earnings.

**Seasonality.** Air travel demand is strongly seasonal (summer leisure
travel, end-of-year holiday travel, weaker shoulder seasons) in a way that
is largely predictable and should be a baseline expectation, not treated as
a surprise catalyst. A quarter-over-quarter comparison that ignores
seasonality can produce a misleading read; year-over-year comparisons are
generally more informative for this sector specifically.

**Capacity discipline.** Because much of an airline's cost base is fixed
once a route is scheduled, the industry is prone to overcapacity in good
times (airlines add routes/frequency chasing demand) which then compresses
fares and margins across the whole sector, not just for the airline that
added capacity. "Capacity discipline" — airlines collectively holding back
on growth even when demand would support it — is a real, watched dynamic;
periods of low discipline tend to precede sector-wide margin compression
even when individual airlines' execution is otherwise sound.

**Balance sheet fragility.** Airlines carry significant fixed costs (aircraft
leases/debt, labor contracts) and thin margins even in good years, which
makes the sector unusually sensitive to demand shocks — a leverage profile
that matters more here than in most sectors when assessing downside risk,
independent of the near-term operational thesis.
