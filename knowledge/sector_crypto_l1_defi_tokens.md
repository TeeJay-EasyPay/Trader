Layer-1 blockchain tokens and DeFi protocol tokens trade on drivers that
have no real equity-market equivalent, and applying equity-style fundamental
analysis unmodified will miss what actually matters for this asset class.

**Protocol adoption metrics.** Rather than revenue or earnings, the closest
analogues are on-chain activity: active addresses, transaction count,
total value locked (TVL) for DeFi protocols, developer activity, and
transaction fee revenue actually paid to the network. These are directly
observable on public blockchains (real, checkable evidence, not
self-reported guidance) and tend to lead price in genuine adoption cycles,
the way user-growth metrics lead revenue in early-stage equities — but they
can also be manipulated or inflated (wash trading for volume/TVL figures,
incentivized but non-organic activity), so the *trend* and *source* of the
metric matters as much as its level.

**Tokenomics and unlock schedules.** Most tokens have a significant portion
of total supply held by the team, early investors, or a foundation treasury,
released on a predetermined vesting/unlock schedule. A large unlock event is
a known, calendar-visible supply increase that can create persistent selling
pressure independent of any change in the protocol's fundamentals — this is
checkable in advance (unlock schedules are typically public) and should be
treated as a real, dateable risk factor, similar to a lock-up expiration in
a newly-listed equity, except usually larger relative to float and
recurring rather than one-time.

**Regulatory and centralization risk.** A meaningful fraction of a token's
value can depend on regulatory treatment (is it deemed a security in a
given jurisdiction, can it be listed on major exchanges, does the protocol
have identifiable founders/entities that can be targeted by enforcement)
in a way most equities do not face directly. Protocols with more
decentralized governance and no clear controlling entity carry different
regulatory risk than protocols effectively controlled by a single company
or foundation, even if their on-chain metrics look similar.

**Liquidity and market structure differ from equities.** Crypto markets
trade continuously (no market open/close, no circuit breakers in most
venues), have highly fragmented liquidity across many exchanges, and can
have significant price differences between a token's liquid pairs and its
thinner ones. Slippage on size is frequently much larger than in a
comparably-capitalized equity, and stop-losses can be triggered by
liquidity gaps (a thin order book letting price gap through a stop level)
more readily than in most listed equities.

**Correlation to the broader crypto market and to BTC specifically** tends
to be high, especially in risk-off moves — a token-specific thesis can
still lose money in a broad crypto drawdown, and this correlation should be
treated as a portfolio-level risk factor (see portfolio risk management),
not diversified away by simply holding several different tokens that in
practice move together most of the time.
