---
title: "Momentum vs. Mean-Reversion"
topics: [momentum, mean_reversion, market_regime]
applies_to: [stock, crypto]

---

Momentum and mean-reversion are opposite bets about the same observation — a
price has moved a meaningful distance from a recent reference point — and
confusing which regime currently applies is one of the most common ways a
otherwise-sound strategy underperforms.

**Momentum** bets that a move continues: an asset that has recently trended
strongly is more likely to keep trending than to reverse, because the move
reflects a real, still-unfolding shift (new information being priced in
over time, sustained buying/selling pressure, or a trend that attracts
further participation, e.g. index-fund flows or trend-following capital).
Momentum tends to dominate over intermediate horizons (weeks to months) and
during periods of clear directional conviction — a genuine narrative
change, an earnings inflection, a structural adoption trend.

**Mean-reversion** bets the opposite: a price that has moved far from a
reference level (a moving average, a recent range, a fair-value estimate)
is more likely to move back toward that reference than to keep extending,
because the move reflects a temporary imbalance (an overreaction, a
liquidity-driven spike, short-term positioning) rather than new durable
information. Mean-reversion tends to dominate over short horizons (intraday
to a few days) and in range-bound, low-conviction markets without a clear
directional catalyst.

**The most common way traders confuse the two**: applying a mean-reversion
read ("this has moved too far, too fast, it must come back") to what is
actually a momentum regime — fading a genuine trend early and repeatedly,
taking small mean-reversion losses while missing the much larger trend move
that eventually follows. The inverse mistake is just as costly: treating an
ordinary, noise-driven overshoot as the start of a durable trend and buying
strength that was always going to revert, entering right before the give-back.

**What actually distinguishes the two regimes in practice**:
- *Catalyst presence.* A move backed by a real, still-developing catalyst
  (news, earnings, a structural narrative, an on-chain adoption metric for
  crypto) is more likely to be momentum. A move with no identifiable
  catalyst — pure price action, thin-volume spikes, forced liquidations — is
  more likely to mean-revert.
- *Volume and participation.* Sustained, broadening volume on the move
  supports momentum continuing; a sharp move on thin, narrow volume is more
  consistent with an overreaction that reverts once the immediate pressure
  passes.
- *Where the move sits relative to a longer-term trend.* A short-term move
  in the direction of an established longer-term trend more often behaves
  like momentum (the longer trend "pulls" it along); a short-term move
  against the longer-term trend more often mean-reverts back toward it.
- *Market-wide regime.* Trending, directional markets (clear risk-on or
  risk-off) favor momentum strategies across most names; choppy, rangebound
  markets with no dominant narrative favor mean-reversion strategies. The
  same setup, applied in the wrong market-wide regime, can go from reliably
  profitable to reliably unprofitable without the setup itself changing at
  all — regime awareness matters as much as setup quality.

A strategy should declare which of the two it is betting on, explicitly, as
part of its thesis — and that declaration should be checked against the
regime evidence above, not assumed.
